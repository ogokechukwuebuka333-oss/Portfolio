Activation Rescue

From days of waiting to instant access. Students and sponsors on Dataset Nexus Tech's learning platform filled in a long enquiry form, then waited for the team to reach out before they could buy a course. I redesigned it into self-service: sign up, pay, start learning.

The problem

A long form, then a wait for someone to follow up. Many people gave up before they ever reached a course, and the company got paid late.

My approach

Ask only for what's needed to get someone learning, take payment in the flow, and let the product, not a person, give access.

The result

Self-service sign-up with Google and Apple sign-in, card checkout and instant course access, in place of an enquiry form of more than 15 fields.

What I owned

  • The redesign of course onboarding, from a manual, sales-led process to self-service
  • The sign-up and sign-in: a short account form in place of a 15+ field enquiry form, with Google and Apple sign-in
  • The course selection and card checkout that lead straight to access
  • The product discovery that surfaced a new core user segment
  • A design system shared across product and marketing pages
Business and user problem

Interest peaked at sign-up, then the wait began

Students, and sponsors paying for them, started with an enquiry form that asked for a lot of detail. Then they waited for the company to reach out before they could get access to a course. Many abandoned before finishing sign-up, and the ones who stayed bought their first course late.

For the business

Revenue arrived late

The first course purchase only happened after a manual follow-up, so money came in slower than it could have.

For the team

Staff time spent chasing

People followed up on every enquiry by hand instead of supporting the students already learning.

For the learner

A form, then silence

A long form to fill in, then a wait with nothing to show for it. The moment they were most interested was the moment they had to wait.

Evidence

Three problems in the old flow, and a user we hadn't designed for

Walking through the old flow step by step showed where it broke: sign up with a long form, wait for outreach, then buy a course. Each step added a reason to leave.

  1. Too much to fill in

    The length of the form alone put people off before they'd seen anything of value.

  2. Nothing happened after signing up

    There was no immediate value: access depended on someone getting in touch.

  3. Payment came last

    Because buying a course waited on the follow-up, the company earned money slower than it could.

Separately, the product discovery I ran surfaced a core user segment the platform hadn't been designed around: startup builders, people learning product skills to build their own thing, rather than only job seekers looking for a first role.

Constraints and trade-offs

Asking for less meant knowing less up front

The long form existed for a reason: it told the team who was enquiring before they followed up. Cutting it was a deliberate trade, which I called a "minimal viable data" approach: ask only for what's needed to give someone access, and nothing more at the door.

What we gave up

Detail about each person, and a conversation with the team, before they bought.

What we gained

Nothing standing between an interested person and their first course, and payment at the moment of interest.

Say what happened to the information the old form collected: was it asked later, dropped, or gathered another way? Add any technical limits (payment provider, course access rules, the platform itself).

Key decisions

Five calls that shaped the new onboarding

  1. Let the product give access, not a person

    No more waiting for outreach. Access is granted automatically, so no one depends on the team to start learning.

  2. A short form at the door, or one tap with Google or Apple

    Name, email, a password and one question about what they're interested in. Or skip the form with Google or Apple. Everything else the old enquiry form asked for, from marital status to salary expectations, stopped being a condition of getting in.

  3. Take payment inside the flow

    People pick a course and pay by card right away, so the first purchase happens while they're interested, not after a follow-up.

  4. Design for startup builders too

    The discovery finding fed into how the platform talks to new learners, how onboarding is framed and how the community is set up.

  5. One design system for product and marketing

    Shared components across the platform and the marketing pages, so what people saw before signing up matched what they got after, and handoff to development had less friction.

Product experience

From an enquiry form to a checkout

The old way in was an enrolment page and a long enquiry form, followed by a wait. The new flow is an account, a course and a payment, with access straight after. Tap any screen to see it in full.

Outcome and validation

Where it landed, and what's still to prove

The manual, sales-led process was replaced by self-service onboarding: a short sign-up or Google and Apple sign-in, a three-step card checkout, and instant access to the course. No one has to be followed up by hand. The discovery work informed a repositioning of the messaging, onboarding and community around the startup-builder segment.

Built into the design

Instant access after payment. A short sign-up, or Google and Apple, in place of the enquiry form. Manual follow-up is gone.

Expected, not yet measured

Fewer people abandoning sign-up, money coming in sooner, less staff time on follow-ups, and more learners finishing courses.

How I'd measure it

Sign-up completion, time from sign-up to first purchase, and course completion by segment (startup builders and job seekers).

Reflection

What this project taught me

Small changes to onboarding can move activation and revenue together. The biggest win here wasn't a screen: it was removing a step that needed a person and putting payment where interest was highest. It also confirmed how I want to work: as a designer, I add the most value by solving the business problem, not just drawing the flow.
