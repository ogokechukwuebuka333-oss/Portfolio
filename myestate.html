The problem

Estates ran dues, records and communication by hand, which made payments slow to reconcile and hard to enforce.

My part

Business and user problem

Dues collected by hand, records nobody trusted

The team's product strategy started from how estates actually run: dues and levies collected manually, reconciliations late or wrong, and committees with no reliable way to enforce payment. Residents, on the other side, had no single place to see what they owed, pay it, or prove they had.

For estate committees

Collecting and enforcing

Matching payments to houses by hand, chasing defaulters, and answering "did my payment arrive?"

For residents

Paying and proving it

Knowing what's due, paying without a trip to the bank, and never having to send a screenshot as proof.

The system

Five roles, each seeing a different product

The complexity is in who can do what. An estate can't go live until the platform approves it, residents can't sign themselves up, and some people can override the gate. These are the roles from the team's requirements.

Constraints and trade-offs

Enforce dues, without locking people out of their homes

The strategy wanted committees to be able to enforce payment through the gate. But a resident who owes dues still has to be able to get home. The rule the team settled on: walking in is always allowed for registered residents, whatever their payment status; driving in can be restricted. The access system had to be fine-grained enough to treat the same person differently at two gates.

Pedestrian gate

Always open to registered residents. No one is shut out of where they live over a bill.

Vehicle gate

Can be tied to payment status, which gives the committee real leverage.

Override

The security manager can admit school buses, delivery trucks or residents in an emergency.

Key decisions

Four calls on the resident side

  1. No self sign-up, no password

    Residents are added by their chairman or secretary, so the house and the person are already linked. They sign in with a fresh SMS code to the number the estate has on file, with nothing to remember.

  2. Show where the money goes

    Every receipt breaks the payment into the estate's share and the platform fee, and says plainly that the resident pays nothing extra.

  3. No proof to send

    After paying, residents are told their chairman and treasurer already see the payment. The screenshot-as-receipt habit is designed out.

  4. Records are permanent

    Payment history can't be edited or deleted, and only the resident, chairman and treasurer can see it. That's what makes it worth trusting in a dispute.

Product experience

From "what do I owe?" to a permanent record

The home screen leads with the total outstanding and what's overdue. From there it's one tap to an invoice, one to pay, and the record is there for good. These are low-fidelity wireframes: they settle structure, content and states, not visual design. Tap any screen to enlarge it.

Edge cases

Designed for when it doesn't go to plan

Most of the resident wireframes are about what happens when something goes wrong, because that's when people lose trust in a payment system.

Debited, but it failed

"Bank shows a debit anyway? Don't pay again." Residents get the reference to give their chairman instead of paying twice.

Estate not set up for online pay

The invoice still shows, with clear instructions to pay by transfer and have the treasurer mark it paid.

Wrong number on file

If the code never arrives, the screen points to the likely cause: the number the chairman has on file.
